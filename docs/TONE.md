# Tone — the north star

This whole project lives or dies on one calibration: **over-the-top and funny,
but always _with_ the accent, never sneering _at_ it.** Get that wrong and it's
mean. Get it right and it's a love letter that happens to be a joke.

## The touchstone

**"Boston Accent,"** Seth Meyers' fake movie-trailer bit from *Late Night with
Seth Meyers* (aired Jan 21, 2016) is the tonal reference for the project's
loudest register. Meyers — a New Hampshire native — plays every Boston-crime-
movie cliché at once. Clip: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rLwbzGyC6t4

What it does that we're copying — in spirit, not in words:

- It parodies a **genre** (the gritty Boston crime drama — the tough-guy,
  hard-neighborhood, everyone's-got-a-past register), not a people. The target
  is the cliché, not the city.
- The **accent itself is the punchline**, cranked past realism into caricature.
- It's **made with obvious affection.** You can tell the people who made it love
  the place. That affection is what buys the exaggeration.

We are not quoting the sketch. We're matching its comedic DNA: affectionate,
self-aware, dialed to eleven, laughing alongside — not down.

## How that maps to the thickness dial

| Level | Vibe | Ship it? |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Light | Real speech, a couple of swaps. | Yes — real content. |
| 2 · Local | Clearly Boston, still readable. | Usually. |
| 3 · Full Masshole | **"Boston Accent" trailer energy** — chowdah-tough, accent to eleven, it's a bit. | No — it's the joke, not the memo. |

## The rule that keeps it a love letter

The joke is **with** people, never **at** them (see `CODE_OF_CONDUCT.md`). When
you run the bit, you go first in the pratfall. The sketch earns its exaggeration
by clearly loving its subject; so do we.
